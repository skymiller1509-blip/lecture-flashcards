Photosynthesis is the process by which green plants convert sunlight, water and carbon dioxide into glucose and oxygen. It takes place mainly in the leaves, inside tiny organelles called chloroplasts. Chlorophyll is the green pigment that absorbs light energy, mostly from the blue and red parts of the spectrum. The light-dependent reactions happen on the thylakoid membranes inside the chloroplast. During these reactions, water molecules are split, releasing oxygen as a by-product. The energy captured from light is stored in two molecules called ATP and NADPH. The second stage is the Calvin cycle, which takes place in the stroma of the chloroplast. In the Calvin cycle, carbon dioxide from the air is fixed into organic molecules using the ATP and NADPH made in the first stage. The main product of the cycle is a three-carbon sugar called glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, which the plant uses to build glucose and starch. Photosynthesis supplies nearly all the oxygen in Earth's atmosphere. It also forms the base of almost every food chain, because plants convert light energy into chemical energy that animals can use. Factors that affect the rate of photosynthesis include light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature. When light intensity is low, the rate is limited by the lack of energy. When carbon dioxide is scarce, the Calvin cycle slows down. Most plants photosynthesise fastest at temperatures between 25 and 35 degrees Celsius, because the enzymes involved work best in that range.
